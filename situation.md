# Uneven Civic Signal Recognition

> A problem situation mapped using Kees Dorst's Frame Creation method.
> Built from 13 cards covering 9 signals across the evidence ladder.

## The situation
A condition where communities struggle to collectively recognize and respond to public issues because attention is filtered through personal relevance, information uncertainty, and perceived social risk.

### Why this is open, not bounded
The situation demands broader public awareness of issues beyond individual experience, but the same conditions that create the need for broader awareness prevent it from working, because people use personal consequence as the filter for deciding what deserves attention.

### Evidence trail
• Evidence: Bounded Public Awareness — Communities experiencing harm may struggle to gain recognition when outsiders rely on narrow geographic, social, or identity-based frames to determine what issues matter. This limits who recognizes the need and reduces broader engagement with the community’s concerns.
  - Signal "Limited Community Awareness": Limited awareness regarding the harm and needs in a community
  - Signal "Local Focus Limitations": Local focus limits who will engage (ex. DC Statehood)
• Theme: Participation Depends on Personal Risk — People deciding whether to engage in public issues weigh participation against perceived personal consequences, including whether an issue affects their lives or exposes them to social harm. When risks feel distant or threatening, they narrow their involvement and withdraw from public action.

  • Pattern: Personal Stakes Drive Participation — People deciding whether to engage with public issues often assess whether those issues have direct consequences for their lives. When an issue feels distant, abstract, or relevant only to others, they are less likely to move from awareness into action, narrowing participation.

    • Evidence: Engagement Requires Personal Relevance — Potential participants evaluate whether an issue applies to them before deciding to act. Issues perceived as affecting only a limited group attract narrower participation, while personally consequential issues change behavior through heightened perceived risk.
      - Signal "Fear Preventing Action": Fear prevents action when consequences are personal
      - Signal "Local Focus Limitations": Local focus limits who will engage (ex. DC Statehood)
    • Evidence: Issues fail to connect — People are not crossing the threshold from “public information” to “personally meaningful concern.”
      - Signal "People Unfamiliar with Issues": People unfamiliar with different issues and not motivated to learn
      - Signal "Low Voter Participation": Low voter participation
  • Pattern: Fear Filters Public Participation — People who consider speaking or acting on public issues filter their engagement through fears of social consequences, especially when negative reactions can spread quickly through social networks. This fear narrows who feels able to participate and what perspectives are expressed.

    • Evidence: Personal Stakes Drive Decisions — People weigh public issues against their own experiences and perceived risks before deciding how to respond. As personal consequences become more salient, they shape both what people believe and whether they take action.

      - Signal "Personal Bias": Bias towards personal experience
      - Signal "Fear Preventing Action": Fear prevents action when consequences are personal
    • Evidence: Fear Silences Participation — People who want to speak or act are constrained when social consequences feel personally threatening. Rapid spread of negative messaging through social media amplifies the perceived risk of public shaming, rejection, or dehumanization, reducing willingness to engage.

      - Signal "Social Media & Negative Messaging": Social media scales the speed in which negative messages spread ie. public shaming or dehumanizing groups
      - Signal "Fear Preventing Action": Fear prevents action when consequences are personal
• Evidence: Fear of Public Consequences — People who consider taking public action may hold back when social consequences feel personally threatening. Negative messaging and rapid public amplification can turn participation into a risk of shame, attack, or reputational harm.

  - Signal "Fear Preventing Action": Fear prevents action when consequences are personal
  - Signal "Social Media & Negative Messaging": Social media scales the speed in which negative messages spread ie. public shaming or dehumanizing groups
• Theme: Civic Capacity Erosion — Individuals trying to understand and participate in public life face declining capacity to process complex information, verify what is credible, and make time for engagement. As cognitive and practical demands increase, people become less confident in their understanding and less likely to participate.
  • Pattern: Unequal Ability to Participate in Public Discourse — people enter civic conversations with uneven knowledge, literacy, and confidence to interpret information.

    • Evidence: Truth Becomes Hard to Access — People are increasingly exposed to information they cannot easily interpret or verify, making it difficult to determine what is accurate. The consequence is uncertainty about which sources and claims deserve trust.
      - Signal "Low Literacy Levels": Low literacy levels and people not able to read deeply
      - Signal "Information Verification & Truth": Verification of information. People don't know #Truth... Facts vs. Fiction, what to believe
    • Evidence: Public Understanding Gap — People may lack the foundational knowledge and reading ability required to make sense of complex issues, creating a disconnect between public conversations and individual understanding. This can leave communities with uneven awareness of issues affecting them.

      - Signal "Low Literacy Levels": Low literacy levels and people not able to read deeply
      - Signal "People Unfamiliar with Issues": People unfamiliar with different issues and not motivated to learn

## Evidence landscape
### What the evidence covers
The key question is whether "signal recognition" captures the deepest mechanism or only one layer.

Dorst would likely push on:

"Are communities failing because they cannot recognize important signals, or because the systems that determine which signals become visible were never designed to represent everyone's concerns equally?"

That distinction matters: the first frames the situation as a sensing problem; the second frames it as a visibility and power problem. Your evidence currently supports both, but does not yet resolve between them.

### Gaps — what I think is still thin
"How does an issue become recognized as a shared concern — and who determines when that happens?"

What is still missing is evidence that distinguishes what kind of breakdown this actually is:

a visibility problem (issues are not reaching people),
an interpretation problem (people cannot make sense of issues),
a trust problem (people cannot determine what to believe),
a participation risk problem (people fear consequences),
or a power problem (some actors determine which issues become visible).

Gap 1 — Whose issues fail to become visible?
Gap 2 — What actually moves people from awareness to participation?
Gap 3 — Is fear a real participation filter or an assumed one?
Gap 4 — Who controls what becomes a civic signal?
Gap 5 — Are people unable to understand information, or unable to trust it?
Gap 6 — Is this a new condition or a longstanding civic pattern?
Gap 7 — Who benefits from uneven civic recognition?

## The paradox
1. The Personal Relevance Paradox — Shared problems must become personal before they become public
The situation demands that people recognize and respond to issues beyond their immediate experience, but the same conditions that make broader recognition necessary prevent it from happening, because civic attention is organized around personal consequence, perceived risk, and individual relevance.

2. The Civic Voice Paradox — Public discourse requires voices that public discourse makes risky.
The situation demands broader participation to create a more informed public conversation, but the same conditions that make participation visible also increase the perceived social risk of participating, causing people to withdraw.

3. The Information Capacity Paradox — More information creates the conditions for less understanding
The situation demands that people become better informed to participate effectively, but the increasing complexity and volume of information makes it harder for people to determine what information is trustworthy and meaningful.

## What comes next
This situation is mapped but not solved — and solving it is not the next step. The next steps in Frame Creation are:

1. **Map the field** — identify every player connected to this situation or a future resolution. Define each by their practices and currency, not their title. Look especially for players who share the values your themes surfaced — even in distant, unexpected domains.
2. **Identify a client** — from the potential problem owners, who could own this situation or adopt a resolution? What would they need to see?
3. **Choose a delivery context** — through what kind of vehicle does a resolution reach the field? (An internal team, a government program, an open-source project, a startup, a nonprofit, a civic institution, a co-op — there is no right answer, only trade-offs.)
4. **Deepen the archaeology** — the gaps above name what's still thin. Go find it: interview people, run deep-research sweeps, pull secondary sources.
5. **Create frames** — only after the field is mapped and the archaeology is deep. "If this situation is approached as if it is ___, then ___."
